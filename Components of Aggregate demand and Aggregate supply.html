Components of Aggregate demand and Aggregate supply

Back to interactive games

Aggregate Demand (AD) is influenced directly by its four components: C + I + G + (X − M).
Each component represents a type of spending, and any change in these at every price level shifts the AD curve.
Open a component to set its factors, then press Shift AD, Shift SRAS or Shift LRAS under the diagram. Use Scenario for output-gap practice.

Consumption factors

Consumption factors do not all shift AD by the same amount. A collapse in consumer confidence has a very large effect. A significant income-tax cut has a large effect. Whereas a small interest-rate change has a small to medium effect.

Investment factors

Investment is about 14–20% of aggregate demand, and its factors do not all shift AD by the same amount. Business confidence and expected profitability have a very large effect. Economic growth and spare capacity have a large effect. Whereas exchange rates have a variable, usually smaller, effect. High spare capacity also discourages investment.

Government spending

Government spending is about 20–25% of aggregate demand, and its drivers do not all shift AD by the same amount. Emergencies, fiscal stimulus and recession spending have a large effect. Automatic stabilisers from unemployment and political priorities have a medium to large effect.

Net exports

Net exports are about −1% to −2% of aggregate demand, because the UK usually buys more from abroad than it sells. Exchange rates are often the most immediate factor. A depreciation raises net exports and shifts AD right. Strong growth in trading partners raises demand for UK exports. Strong domestic growth can raise imports and weaken net exports.

Exchange rates +0.06
Growth in trading partners +0.04
Domestic economic growth +0.03

LRAS

Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) is influenced by anything that changes the economy’s productive capacity — the quantity or quality of its factors of production. These factors do not all shift LRAS by the same amount. Productivity and technology have a very high effect. Capital and infrastructure have a high effect. Available labour has a medium to high effect. Natural resources have a low to medium effect.

Aggregate demand and supply

AD, SRAS and LRAS meet at full-employment output, Yfe.

Consumption is the largest component of AD — around 60% of UK AD.
When consumption rises, households spend more, so AD shifts right.
When consumption falls, AD shifts left.

Investment is spending by firms on capital goods (machines, buildings, technology).
It is volatile and strongly influenced by expectations.
Higher investment → AD shifts right
Lower investment → AD shifts left

Government spending is a direct injection into the economy.
Large effects come from emergencies, fiscal stimulus and recession spending.
Higher unemployment raises welfare spending through automatic stabilisers.
Higher G → AD shifts right
Lower G → AD shifts left

Net exports = exports minus imports.
A depreciation of the pound typically raises exports and reduces imports, so AD shifts right.
Strong growth abroad raises demand for UK exports.
Strong domestic growth can raise imports and weaken net exports.

Better training, technology, management and organisation allow firms to produce more at the same cost.
Productivity improvements shift SRAS right.
Falling productivity shifts SRAS left.
Impact: large.

If inputs become more expensive, firms can supply less at each price level.
Oil price rises → transport & production costs rise → SRAS left
Commodity prices fall → cheaper inputs → SRAS right

Energy is a major cost for manufacturing, transport, and services.
Higher electricity/gas prices → SRAS left
Cheaper energy (e.g., renewables, subsidies) → SRAS right

Wages are the biggest cost for most firms.
Higher wages → higher costs → SRAS shifts left
Lower wages → lower costs → SRAS shifts right

Anything that increases the cost of compliance or production shifts SRAS left.
Higher corporation tax → SRAS left
More regulation (e.g., environmental rules) → SRAS left
Tax cuts or deregulation → SRAS right

A depreciation makes imported inputs more expensive, so firms’ costs rise and SRAS shifts left.
An appreciation makes imported inputs cheaper, so SRAS shifts right.

Subsidies shift SRAS to the right because they reduce firms’ production costs in the short run.

Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) is influenced by anything that changes the economy’s productive capacity — the quantity or quality of its factors of production.
Productivity and technology have a very high effect. Capital and infrastructure have a high effect.
Not demand side factors.
