Stagflation

Back to interactive games · Conflict of objectives: Inflation and unemployment

Stagflation is high inflation and high unemployment together, with low economic growth and a negative output gap.
A policy that shifts aggregate demand left can cut inflation, but unemployment rises and growth weakens.
A policy that shifts aggregate demand right can cut unemployment, but inflation rises.
Supply-side policy acts on costs. Press for the scenario.

Scenario

Economic scenario

An energy-price shock and higher import costs have raised firms' costs of production. Short-run aggregate supply has shifted left. Full-employment output is £3,040 billion. Real output has fallen to 5% below that, so the negative output gap is £152 billion. Unemployment is 8%. Inflation is 8%. Economic growth is −1.5%.

The inflation is cost-push. It has not been caused by excess demand. The economy has high inflation and high unemployment at the same time, with falling output. This is stagflation.

The Bank of England base rate is 4%. The government is running a budget deficit. The pound is weak, so imported energy stays expensive.

Your task

Bring the economy to all four of these results:

Read the negative output gap from the diagram first. Then choose policies. A tighter monetary policy shifts AD left and trades lower inflation for higher unemployment. Targeted support for energy costs, supply-side policy, a stronger pound and energy security can shift SRAS right, so output can rise while the price level falls.

Policy choices

Cost-push stagflation occurs when a negative supply shock causes both inflation and unemployment to rise at the same time. For example, a sharp increase in energy prices raises firms' production costs. As a result, short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) shifts left.
